Blind review's usual justification is that it removes bias about who wrote
the thing. It does some of that. What it more reliably does is remove the
social cost of saying the specific thing --- a reviewer who'd soften a note
to a colleague's face will often write the same note, unsoftened, when the
byline is hidden. That's not a story about honesty; it's a story about power,
and about how much of a specific note gets quietly rounded down to protect a
relationship rather than to protect the truth of the claim.

It doesn't fix the underlying skill. An anonymous vague note is still vague
--- anonymity removes the cost of specificity, it doesn't manufacture the
specificity itself. And it introduces a new failure: a reviewer who knows
anonymity is temporary, or who can guess the author anyway, can use the mask
as licence for exactly the un-falsifiable cruelty this course spends every
other week trying to train out. Anonymity is a tool for one specific
problem --- the social cost of saying a true, specific thing to a known
person --- and it's worth noticing when it's being used for something else.
